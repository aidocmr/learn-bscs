### How is Philosophy Defined in Relation to the Self?

Philosophy (_Philo_ = loving, _Sophia_ = wisdom) is the "mother of all disciplines." It defines the self as a unified being connected to consciousness, awareness, and rational choice (agency).

### What are the Classical Antiquity Views (Socrates, Plato, Aristotle)?

- **Socrates**: _"The unexamined life is not worth living."_ Dualism of mortal body and immortal soul.

- **Plato**: _"The first and best victory is to conquer self."_ Tripartite soul (Reason, Physical Appetite, Will/Spirit).

- **Aristotle**: _"Knowing yourself is the beginning of all wisdom."_ Mind/soul and body are co-dependent; soul gives form/essence to matter.

### How Did Medieval and Modern Philosophers View the Self?

- **St. Augustine**: Sense of self is defined through its relationship with God.

- **René Descartes**: _"Cogito ergo sum"_ (I think, therefore I am). The self is a thinking entity (_res cogitans_) separate from the body.

- **John Locke**: Tabula Rasa (blank slate). Consciousness and memory define identity.

- **David Hume**: Bundle Theory—there is no permanent, enduring self, only a continuous stream of perceptions.

- **Immanuel Kant**: We actively construct the self by organizing sensory data using reason.

### What are the Contemporary Philosophical Views?

- **Sigmund Freud**: Mind split into Conscious and Unconscious (governed by pleasure principle).

- **Gilbert Ryle**: _"The self is how you behave."_ Behaviorist perspective.

- **Paul Churchland**: Eliminative Materialism—the self is the physical brain, not an imaginary mind.

- **Maurice Merleau-Ponty**: Embodied Subjectivity—mind and body are unified in lived experience.

## Summary

Philosophy traces the evolution of self-concept from classical dualism (soul vs. body) to modern cognitive and physicalist perspectives. While rationalists like Descartes and early thinkers prioritized the mind or soul, empiricists like Locke and Hume highlighted experience and sensory impressions. Modern physicalist and phenomenological models (Churchland, Merleau-Ponty) unite mental experience directly with neurological and bodily existence.

## Main Notes

### Classical Antiquity

- **Socrates**: Soul is immortal and holds knowledge; body is imperfect/mortal. Ignorance comes from forgetting truth upon entering the material world.

- **Plato**: Tripartite soul:
  - _Reason_: Wise choices, thinking.
  - _Appetite_: Biological needs (hunger, thirst).
  - _Will/Spirit_: Passion, anger, empathy.

- **Aristotle**: Rejects complete separation of soul/body. Soul is the _essence_ (meaning), body is the _matter_ (substance).

### Middle Ages & Early Modern Era

- **St. Augustine**: Self realized through God's transcendent love, combining faith and reason.

- **René Descartes**: Dualist founder of modern philosophy. Non-material thinking mind vs. material physical body.

- **John Locke**: Mind starts as _Tabula Rasa_. Personal identity relies on memory and conscious awareness over time.

- **David Hume**: Skeptic/Empiricist. Self is an illusion—a "bundle of fleeting perceptions" with no constant underlying entity.

### Enlightenment to Modern Era

- **Immanuel Kant**: Synthesis of rationalism/empiricism. Mind actively unifies sensory data to construct selfhood. Inner self (rational/psychological) vs. outer self (bodily representation).

- **Sigmund Freud**: Unconscious mind (primitive drives, pleasure principle) dominates behavior over conscious awareness.

- **Gilbert Ryle**: Rejects "ghost in the machine." Self is defined by observable behavioral patterns and dispositions.

- **Paul Churchland**: Materialism/Neurophilosophy. Self is entirely tied to physical brain physiology.

- **Maurice Merleau-Ponty**: Phenomenology. Consciousness, body, and sensory perception form an integrated "embodied subjectivity."
