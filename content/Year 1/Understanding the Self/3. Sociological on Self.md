### What is Sociology?

The study of how society and social structures shape individual and group behavior.

### What is Socialization?

The continuous process of learning one's culture, adapting to societal norms, and adjusting behavior to fit group expectations.

### How is the Self a Social Construction?

Self-identity develops through social interaction, external feedback, and roles assigned by agents of socialization.

### What are the key Social Agencies?

- **Family**: Most pervasive and influential across life stages.
- **Schools**: Broadens worldviews, develops skills, and fosters civic responsibility.
- **Community**: Includes peers, workplaces, mass media, religion, and government.

### What is Mead's "I" vs. "Me" theory?

George Herbert Mead split the self into a dynamic, two-part relationship:

- **"Me"**: The socialized self reflecting learned societal expectations and attitudes.
- **"I"**: The active, spontaneous identity responding to the "Me".

### What is Cooley's Looking-Glass Self?

Charles Horton Cooley proposed that people use others as a "mirror," constructing self-worth and identity based on how they believe others perceive and judge them.

### Material vs. Non-Material Culture

- **Material**: Physical creations and technology.
- **Non-Material**: Intangible ideas, beliefs, values, and norms.

### What are the 5 Elements of Culture?

1. **Knowledge**: Practical info from experience/study.
2. **Social Norms**: Standard group expectations.
3. **Folkways**: Casual customs and general social habits.
4. **Mores**: Deeply held moral standards and sanctions.
5. **Laws**: Formally enacted rules enforced by government.

## Summary

Individual identity is not formed in isolation; it is a social construct shaped by culture and key agents of socialization like family, schools, and peer networks. Through George Herbert Mead's distinction between the socialized "Me" and reactive "I," and Charles Horton Cooley's Looking-Glass Self model, sociology demonstrates that human self-worth and behavior are continually molded by perceived external expectations. Culture provides the structure for this development across both tangible tools (material) and regulating societal rules ranging from everyday folkways to enforced laws (non-material).
